POINT OF INTEREST

Jezero Crater

Features of the Jezero Crater:
With a diameter of approximately 45 km, the crater floor lies at an elevation of -2,600 m relative to the surface of Mars and features various types of terrain: Crater rim: This consists of elevated features that define the circular structure. It is characterized by steep slopes and significant changes in elevation, making it a difficult area to traverse. Crater floor: This is the interior region, situated at a lower altitude than the surrounding terrain. It contains relatively flat areas, alongside irregularities, sedimentary deposits, and rocky outcrops. Western delta: This is one of the primary geomorphological features of Jezero. It was formed by the accumulation of sediments transported by ancient watercourses. It exhibits stepped surfaces, stratified deposits, and variations in elevation. Fluvial channels: Ancient inflow and outflow channels have been identified, which once connected the crater to the surrounding regions. These structures provide evidence of the flow of liquid water on Mars in the past. Slopes and elevation changes: The terrain features variations in incline that must be taken into account when assessing vehicle mobility and the safety of movement. Steep slopes and rocky surfaces represent potential obstacles.

CONSIDERATIONS

PHYSICAL HEALTH

The following health issues must be considered as a baseline:

Muscle mass loss and bone weakness resulting from reduced gravity:

The body no longer supports its usual weight, causing muscle atrophy and bone demineralization. A solution could be practicing mandatory daily resistance and impact exercise routines.

Exposure to space radiation:

Exposure to galactic cosmic radiation and solar particle events damages cellular DNA, increasing the risk of cancer. One solution is rover shielding.

Martian dust toxicity:

The dust contains perchlorates and silicates that affect the thyroid gland and can cause fibrosis or lung damage. One solution is the use of suitports (docked suits) and ventilation filters.

Cardiovascular and fluid alterations:

Without gravity, fluids redistribute toward the upper body, increasing intracranial pressure. One solution is the use of lower body negative pressure (LBNP) trousers to draw fluids down toward the legs.

Physical strain and trauma:

Constantly working inside a pressurized suit generates extreme muscle fatigue. One solution is designing suits with low-friction joints.

Circadian rhythm disruption:

The accumulated daily lag relative to Earth's biological clock desynchronizes melatonin and cortisol, causing chronic fatigue, cognitive impairment, and a weakened immune system. One solution is circadian LED lighting inside the rover.

Nutrition:

The diet will rely on thermostabilized foods in which certain vitamins degrade over time. One solution is personalized nutritional supplementation.

SOIL CHEMISTRY

On-foot traverses will be pursued to enable access to narrow zones and allow for millimetric observation of microfissures, rocky outcrops, and delta walls.

Soil chemistry varies between volcanic activity and liquid water episodes from millions of years ago. The following can be found:

Olivine and Pyroxenes:

Their abundance indicates igneous rocks with minimal water alteration.

Clays:

Formed when liquid water chemically alters silicates over extended periods; they trap and preserve organic matter or microstructures.

Carbonates:

Acting as "preserving" agents, they record the chemistry of ancient lakes and preserve biosignatures (similar to fossilization).

Sulfates:

Mineral salts that precipitate when sulfur-rich bodies of water evaporate, indicating climatic shifts toward arid or saline environments.

Perchlorates:

Oxidizing and toxic salts dispersed throughout the surface dust that help identify the planet's redox chemistry.

Traces of organic carbon:

Aid in the search for evidence of past life.

SAMPLING METHODOLOGY

Contamination or alteration of the chemical integrity of the sample is avoided in the following manner:

Core extraction:

Use of a drill with a diamond or carbide bit to extract intact rock cylinders while preserving the mineral microstructure.

Surface regolith collection:

Sterile trowels and brushes are used to capture fine dust, hydrated salt crusts, and sediments.

Hermetic sealing and storage:

Samples are immediately placed into airtight storage tubes to prevent oxidation by the Martian atmosphere and the loss of volatile compounds or trapped moisture.

Cross-contamination chain:

The astronaut uses tools with interchangeable or plasma/heat-sterilizable tips between each sampling site to avoid biasing biosignature analyses.

RADIATION

Outside Earth's magnetic field, prolonged exposure to radiation is incompatible with human life, and Mars's magnetic field is much weaker than Earth's.

We highlight the danger of galactic cosmic rays, which are high-energy particles capable of penetrating several meters into the Martian soil. We consider that solar energetic particles vary across different orders of magnitude:

Mild events:

They lose almost all of their energy upon reaching the surface.

Hard events:

They produce a substantial flux on the Martian surface.

Special use will be made of pressurized vehicles that allow explorers to remain inside for weeks without needing to wear any spacesuits, specifically built for long-duration missions. These must be characterized by:

Autonomy:

14 to 30 days with a crew of 2 to 4 passengers.

Integrated laboratory and life-support supplies:

Capacity to carry a fully integrated laboratory and all vital life-support supplies for the expedition.

Speed:

A cruising speed of 8 km/h and a top speed of 20 km/h.

Terrain capability:

The capability to traverse terrains where Martian geography is hostile.

Energy systems:

Powered by energy systems allowing 50 km trips via solar power, or a radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG) for much longer missions.

MENTAL HEALTH

Psychological well-being is essential to maintain adaptability, concentration, and the group functioning of the 4 crew members during the Mars exploration.

1. Pre-flight psychological evaluation:

An individual psychological evaluation must be carried out for each crew member, assessing adaptability, stress management, frustration tolerance, and coping skills. Social skills, communication, and conflict resolution will also be evaluated.

2. Daily well-being:

Rest schedules must be maintained and excessive work shifts avoided to prevent sleep disruptions, fatigue, and stress.

3. Psychological care:

It is necessary to maintain communication with professionals on Earth at regular intervals, conducting both individual and group monitoring, with brief daily evaluations regarding mood, stress levels, sleep quality, interpersonal friction, and attention, memory, and concentration. Action protocols must also be in place for situations involving intense stress or emotional difficulties.
